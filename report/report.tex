\section{Introduction}

Sensitive-data leakage through organizational email is a long-standing
security challenge, and the growing use of generative AI adds a new
channel in which users may expose personal, financial, or confidential
content in free-form text, extending the traditional Data Loss
Prevention (DLP) problem. Detection is difficult because sensitive
information has no uniform textual form: structured identifiers such as
credit-card or social-security numbers follow recognizable patterns,
while other disclosures depend on linguistic context or appear in
modified formats (the same phone number written as a continuous digit
sequence or separated by spaces and hyphens), and sensitive concepts
may be mentioned in instructions, negations, or hypothetical scenarios
without revealing actual information. No single detection approach
handles all of these forms reliably.

In this work, we formulate sensitive-information detection as a
multi-label classification problem over short texts, distinguishing
benign content, general PII, financial information, and confidential
information. We evaluate four approaches with different capabilities: a
rule-based detector combining regular expressions and named-entity
recognition for known structured patterns, a BiLSTM with
character-level convolutional features for variations in the internal
structure of sensitive tokens, and two fine-tuned transformer
classifiers (BERT and RoBERTa) that model the surrounding linguistic
context.

While email DLP is the motivating application, it is not the evaluated
task: all quantitative results are obtained on the
\texttt{ai4privacy/pii-masking-300k} dataset \citep{ai4privacy2024}, a
corpus of short synthetic PII-bearing sentences that lacks the
structure of real email (subject lines, threading, quoting,
signatures). Our results therefore measure sensitive-\emph{sentence}
classification, an external-validity limitation we return to in the
Discussion.

Our study is organized around two research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} Does a multi-model ensemble outperform the best
    single model for sensitive-content classification?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} Does model complementarity vary across
    sensitivity categories (PII vs.\ financial vs.\ confidential)?
\end{itemize}

To answer these questions, we combine the four models into an ensemble
that averages their per-category probabilities under a separate set of
learned weights for each category. This weighting scheme is standard in
the classifier-combination literature
\citep{kittler1998combining, kuncheva2004combining}, and we do not
present it as new. The contribution lies elsewhere: (i) a rigorously
significance-gated empirical finding --- the ensemble does beat the
best single model, with a delta whose 95\% confidence interval excludes
zero under a paired bootstrap and survives a Holm--Bonferroni
correction against every member, but the gain is practically small (a
few tenths of a macro-F1 point), and we report effect size next to
significance so a tiny-but-real gain is never dressed up as a large
one; (ii) a mechanistic explanation of \emph{why} the gain is small ---
a leave-one-model-out ablation shows the fitted ensemble's reliance is
concentrated on a single member rather than cleanly split across
categories, inherently limiting the headroom over that member alone;
and (iii) a reusable, honesty-first evaluation methodology for judging
ensemble claims --- significance gate, multi-comparison panel,
ablation, operating-point view, and cost accounting.

\subsection{Related Work}

Sensitive-information detection has traditionally relied on regular
expressions, dictionaries, and named-entity recognition: rule-based
de-identification achieved high recall on protected health information
in clinical text \citep{neamatullah2008deid}, pattern-matching methods
dominated early de-identification research \citep{meystre2010deid}, and
modern tools such as Microsoft Presidio \citep{presidio} continue this
line by combining regular-expression recognizers with NER. Because
predefined patterns miss context-dependent or irregular disclosures,
later work framed enterprise DLP as supervised text classification
\citep{hart2011dlp}, applied deep learning to context-dependent
sensitive information \citep{ahmed2021sensitive}, combined regular
expressions with a BERT-BiLSTM-Attention extractor
\citep{guo2021exsense}, and used transformers
\citep{devlin2019bert, liu2019roberta} for sensitive-data detection,
including BERT-based anonymization of clinical text
\citep{garciapablos2020bert}.

Combining classifiers to exploit complementary behavior is a classical
topic: weighted soft voting and linear opinion pooling date back to the
multiple-classifier-systems literature \citep{kittler1998combining},
per-class combiner weights are well established
\citep{kuncheva2004combining}, more expressive alternatives include
stacked generalization \citep{wolpert1992stacked} and
mixture-of-experts gating \citep{jacobs1991adaptive}, and in
multi-label settings weighted ensembles can assign per-label importance
to individual classifiers \citep{xia2021multilabel}. Our fusion rule is
an instance of per-class weighted soft voting; what we add is an
empirical comparison of pattern-aware, character-aware, and
context-aware models within a common sensitive-text classification
framework, using the learned per-category weights and ablations to
characterize complementarity across sensitivity categories.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{General Approach}
\label{subsec:general-approach}

Sensitive information can appear as a recognizable structured pattern,
an irregular or obfuscated sequence, or a disclosure whose sensitivity
depends on the surrounding context. We therefore evaluate four models
--- a rule-based detector, a BiLSTM with character-level features, and
two fine-tuned transformers (BERT and RoBERTa) --- each predicting, for
an input text, whether it is benign and whether it contains general
personally identifiable information (PII), financial information, or
confidential information. Since the models capture different textual
signals, their predictions are combined using category-specific
confidence-weighted late fusion, and the individual models and the
ensemble are compared using per-category metrics, confidence intervals,
statistical significance testing, error analysis, and model ablation.

\subsection{Data Preparation and Label Construction}
\label{subsec:data-preparation}

The dataset provides each text together with a \texttt{privacy\_mask}
containing the sensitive entities found in it. We convert these
entity-level annotations into a four-dimensional text-level label
vector $\mathbf{y} = [y_{\text{benign}}, y_{\text{PII}},
y_{\text{financial}}, y_{\text{confidential}}]$: a text is benign when
it contains no annotated sensitive entity; any sensitive entity
activates the PII label; financial identifiers (social-security numbers
and card-related entities) additionally activate the financial label;
and passwords and identity-document identifiers (passport, ID card,
driver's licence) activate the confidential label. The labels are
hierarchical --- financial and confidential are subsets of PII, and
benign is mutually exclusive with all sensitive categories --- so a
single text may belong to more than one sensitive category.

We note that the labels are \emph{derived} from the dataset's entity
annotations rather than independently annotated: the ground truth is a
deterministic function of the entity mask, and part of what the models
learn is to recover this mapping. This is appropriate for comparing
model families on a shared task, but it should be kept in mind when
interpreting absolute scores, and it partly explains why pattern-based
models are competitive on categories defined by regex-shaped entities.

After filtering to English, the data contain 29{,}908 training and
7{,}946 validation examples. We reserve the dataset's
\texttt{validation} split exclusively for final reporting (TEST) and
carve a seeded 10\% slice out of the training split
(\texttt{train\_holdout}) as the development set (DEV) for model
selection, ensemble-weight learning, and threshold tuning. All models
share the same preprocessing and label-construction functions, so their
predictions are evaluated against identical ground truth.

\subsection{Individual Models}
\label{subsec:individual-models}

\subsubsection{Rule-Based Detector}
\label{subsubsec:rule-based}

The rule-based detector is the pattern-aware component. It uses
Microsoft Presidio \citep{presidio}, which combines regular-expression
recognizers with spaCy named-entity recognition, and identifies
entities such as email addresses, phone numbers, credit-card numbers,
bank identifiers, social-security numbers, passports, and driver's
licences. Because stock Presidio has no recognizers for several
confidential entity types in our schema, we added three custom
recognizers covering password-like secrets, generic identity-document
numbers, and classification-marker vocabulary; detected entities are
mapped to the four-label schema through the same entity mapping used
for ground-truth construction. The model requires no training and
provides transparent detections, but is expected to be less effective
when sensitive information is obfuscated, implicit, or
context-dependent.

\subsubsection{BiLSTM with Character-Level Features}
\label{subsubsec:bilstm}

The BiLSTM model is the character-aware component, intended to
recognize variations that fixed rules miss --- unusual separators,
formatting changes, and out-of-vocabulary tokens. Each token combines a
frozen pre-trained 100-dimensional GloVe word embedding
\citep{pennington2014glove} with a character-level representation
produced by convolutional filters of different sizes, which learn local
structural patterns within words and identifiers. The concatenated
representation passes through a two-layer bidirectional LSTM
\citep{hochreiter1997lstm}, mean pooling over non-padding positions,
and a linear layer with four sigmoid outputs giving independent
per-label probabilities. The model is trained for 20 epochs with batch
size 64 and learning rate $10^{-3}$, keeping the checkpoint with the
best validation loss.

\subsubsection{Fine-Tuned Transformers: BERT and RoBERTa}
\label{subsubsec:transformers}

BERT \citep{devlin2019bert} and RoBERTa \citep{liu2019roberta} are the
context-aware components, intended to capture information that fixed
patterns cannot express, such as implicit disclosures and mentions in
instructions, negations, or hypotheticals. Texts are tokenized with
each model's native tokenizer (\texttt{bert-base-uncased} WordPiece and
\texttt{roberta-base} BPE) and truncated to 512 tokens; the contextual
representation of the initial classification token feeds a linear layer
producing four sigmoid-activated label probabilities. To reduce
computational cost, the embedding layer and first ten transformer
blocks are frozen and only the final two blocks and the classification
head are fine-tuned --- trading a small amount of accuracy for a
training footprint feasible on our hardware. Training uses binary
cross-entropy with logits, positive-class weighting, label smoothing,
gradient clipping, and a learning-rate schedule with warmup.

\subsection{Confidence-Weighted Late Fusion}
\label{subsec:fusion}

Each model produces a probability $p_{m,l}(x)$ for model $m$, label
$l$, and input text $x$. The ensemble combines these probabilities
separately for each label:

\begin{equation}
    p_{l}^{\text{ensemble}}(x)
    =
    \frac{
        \sum_{m} w_{m,l} \, p_{m,l}(x)
    }{
        \sum_{m} w_{m,l}
    },
    \label{eq:weighted-fusion}
\end{equation}

where $w_{m,l}$ is the weight assigned to model $m$ for label $l$.
Unlike uniform averaging, this lets the same model have different
importance across sensitivity categories --- e.g., the rule-based
detector for structured financial identifiers, a transformer for
context-dependent PII.

The category-specific weights are fit on the DEV split by a per-label
grid search refined with Nelder--Mead optimization of the true
per-label F1. Per-label decision thresholds are tuned on the same DEV
split and applied unchanged when reporting on TEST. We deliberately
chose this simple linear pooling over stacking \citep{wolpert1992stacked}
or input-conditioned gating \citep{jacobs1991adaptive}: with only four
members it is less prone to overfitting the small development split,
and --- central to RQ2 --- the learned weights remain directly
interpretable as a measure of how much each category relies on each
model family.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:evaluation}

All models are evaluated with a shared framework on the untouched TEST
split. We report per-category precision, recall, and F1, macro-F1, and
ROC-AUC, with a 95\% bootstrap confidence interval for macro-F1
(1{,}000 resamples, fixed seed). For RQ1, a paired bootstrap over TEST
examples tests whether the ensemble's macro-F1 gain over the best
single model is significant, extended to a pairwise panel against
\emph{every} member with a Holm--Bonferroni correction so the verdict
is not an artifact of picking one comparison; effect sizes (deltas with
confidence intervals) are reported alongside p-values throughout, and
model stability is assessed with 5-fold cross-validation. We also
analyze errors associated with negation, hypothetical language,
implicit disclosure, and obfuscation --- descriptively only, since the
dataset provides no controlled contrast set, so no robustness claim is
attached. Finally, a leave-one-model-out ablation, together with the
learned per-category weights, is used to answer RQ2: whether each model
provides a distinct contribution, and to which categories.

\section{Experimental results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Settings}
\label{subsec:settings}

All numbers below are computed on the untouched TEST split (7{,}946
English examples), with ensemble weights and decision thresholds fit on
DEV and applied unchanged: 0.80 (benign), 0.10 (PII), 0.50 (financial),
0.55 (confidential). Throughout, \emph{headline macro-F1} averages F1
over the three informative categories (PII, financial, confidential);
\texttt{benign} is reported per label but excluded from the macro,
since it is the complement of PII and would double-count the same
decision. Confidence intervals use bootstrap resampling (1{,}000
resamples, fixed seed).

The learned weights (Table~\ref{tab:weights}) \emph{look} like
per-category specialization: RoBERTa heavy on benign, PII, and
confidential, with financial spread across three members. The ablation
tells a different story. Removing RoBERTa costs about 1.5 macro-F1
points ($0.974 \rightarrow 0.959$; benign $0.927 \rightarrow 0.765$
per-label), while removing \emph{any other} member barely moves the
score (all remaining configurations stay at $0.970$ or above). PII
stays at $\approx 0.990$ under every single removal, and financial ---
the one category with spread weights --- shows only mild reliance:
dropping the BiLSTM leaves financial at $0.948$, essentially tied with
RoBERTa alone. The answer to RQ2 is therefore a \textbf{qualified no}:
the fitted ensemble's real reliance is concentrated on RoBERTa almost
everywhere, not cleanly differentiated by category (note this measures
reliance under the fixed DEV-fit weights, not intrinsic
complementarity). This concentration also explains the RQ1 result
mechanistically: an ensemble dominated by one member is essentially
that member plus a little, so the headroom to beat it is inherently
small --- exactly the reliably-positive-but-tiny gain the paired
bootstrap measures.

\subsection{DLP Operating Point}
\label{subsec:operating-point}

Headline macro-F1 uses F1-optimal thresholds, but a deployed DLP system
runs at high recall, since a missed sensitive item costs far more than a
false alarm. Figure~\ref{fig:pr-curves} shows the ensemble's
precision--recall curves per category. At a recall floor of 0.99, the
ensemble attains the best per-category precision of any system on all
three informative categories --- 0.993 (PII), 0.621 (financial), 0.964
(confidential) --- each at least matching the best single model; on
benign, BERT (0.489) beats the ensemble (0.427). Three caveats keep
this a supporting qualifier rather than an independent claim: the
recall-floor thresholds are selected on the TEST split itself
(optimistic upper bounds), the edge is not significance-tested, and the
benign and financial values sit in low-precision regions where
estimates are noisy.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{RQ1: yes, but small.} The ensemble does outperform a single
fine-tuned RoBERTa, and the win holds against every member under a Holm
correction --- but the gain over the transformers is a few tenths of a
macro-F1 point, purchased at roughly 2$\times$ the inference cost.
Statistical significance here does not imply operational significance:
reported honestly, the finding is a gain that is reliably positive but
practically small.

\paragraph{RQ2: a qualified no.} The learned weights suggest
per-category specialization, but the ablation shows the fitted
ensemble's real reliance is concentrated on RoBERTa almost everywhere
--- which is also the mechanism behind RQ1's small margin. The one
place the ensemble shows a broader edge, best per-category precision on
all informative categories at the recall~$\geq 0.99$ operating point,
is reported with its caveats (test-split thresholds, no significance
test) as a supporting qualifier rather than an independent result.
